## How to identify manual processes costing your business money

Manual work is not always a problem. Some tasks are better handled by people.

The expensive manual processes are the ones that happen repeatedly, involve low-value administration and quietly consume time across the team without anyone measuring it.

The cost is rarely obvious because it is spread across five minutes here, ten minutes there and dozens of small handoffs throughout the week.

The easiest way to find these processes is to look for a few recurring signals.

## 1. Repeated copy and paste work

If staff regularly copy information from one system into another, that is one of the clearest signs of an automation or integration opportunity.

Examples include:

- website enquiry → CRM
- CRM → accounting software
- spreadsheet → job management system
- email → internal tracker
- customer record → quote document

The problem is not only the time spent copying.

Every manual transfer also creates another opportunity for information to be missed, entered differently or left out of date. We look at that hidden cost in more detail in [the hidden cost of tools that don't talk to each other](/blog/disconnected-business-tools).

### What to measure

Track how many times the task happens each week and how long each transfer takes.

A two-minute task performed once is irrelevant. A two-minute task repeated hundreds of times becomes a process worth investigating.

## 2. Work that depends on someone remembering

A process becomes fragile when the next step happens only because a person remembers to do it.

Common examples include:

- following up an unanswered quote
- telling operations that a sale has closed
- checking whether a document has been approved
- notifying a customer about a status change
- updating another system after a job is completed

These are not necessarily difficult tasks. The problem is that they compete with more important work for attention.

A reliable system should trigger predictable actions when the relevant event occurs.

## 3. Duplicate data entry

Duplicate entry usually means systems are not sharing information properly.

If customer details are entered into a CRM, then typed again into accounting software and again into an operations system, the business is paying several times to maintain the same information.

It also creates a second problem: which system is correct when the records disagree?

Before automating anything, decide which system should own each type of information. Then work out where that information genuinely needs to flow.

For a real example, [CorIT Tech](/case-studies/corit-tech) made its CRM the central source of customer information, so projects and tasks are created from it rather than retyped. CorIT reported saving more than 10 hours of administrative work a month.

## 4. Frequent corrections and rework

A process that regularly requires staff to fix data, resend documents or correct mistakes has a hidden cost beyond the initial task.

Look for comments such as:

- "I have to check this every time."
- "The spreadsheet is usually wrong."
- "We need to update both systems."
- "Someone forgot to add it."
- "We have to rebuild the report if the numbers change."

Those statements often point directly to process problems.

The best fix may be automation, but it may also be better validation, clearer ownership or removing an unnecessary step.

## 5. Slow handoffs between people or teams

Many processes are fast while someone is actively working on them and slow while they are waiting for the next person.

For example (illustrative):

- A quote may take twenty minutes to prepare but two days to get approved because the approval request sits in an inbox.
- A customer onboarding task may take ten minutes but wait until the following morning because operations was never notified.

Map the waiting time as well as the working time.

Automation can often help by creating tasks, sending notifications, routing approvals and escalating items that sit untouched for too long.

## 6. Shadow spreadsheets

A shadow spreadsheet is a spreadsheet the team maintains because the official systems do not give them the view or workflow they need.

One spreadsheet is not automatically a problem.

The warning signs are when:

- several people edit it
- it controls an operational process
- information is copied into it from other systems
- the same data also exists elsewhere
- formulas are fragile
- there is uncertainty about which version is current
- reporting depends on one person's knowledge of how it works

At that point, the spreadsheet is no longer just a convenience. It has become infrastructure.

The right response may be to connect it, improve it, move the process into an existing platform or replace it with a small custom application. Our guide on [how to replace spreadsheet-based business processes](/blog/replace-spreadsheet-business-processes) walks through those options.

## 7. Customer updates that require manual chasing

Customers often ask for updates because the business process does not provide them automatically.

Examples include:

- "Has my quote been approved?"
- "Has the job been booked?"
- "Has the order shipped?"
- "Did you receive my documents?"
- "What happens next?"

If staff repeatedly look up the same status information and send the same type of response, there may be an opportunity to automate routine updates while keeping exceptions personal.

This is particularly useful when the update is based on a real system event rather than a generic scheduled message.

## Calculate the annual cost of a manual process

You do not need a complex ROI model to get a useful estimate.

Use this formula:

**Annual manual-process cost = time per occurrence × number of occurrences × loaded hourly staff cost**

Fill in your own numbers:

- time per occurrence, in minutes
- occurrences per week
- staff involved
- loaded hourly cost, in A$
- working weeks per year

Then calculate:

**(minutes ÷ 60) × occurrences per week × working weeks × hourly cost**

If several people touch the process, calculate each role separately and add them together.

This does not capture every cost. It ignores delays, errors, missed opportunities and customer frustration. But it gives you a useful baseline for deciding whether the process is worth improving.

## Add the cost of errors and delays

Time is only one part of the problem.

Ask:

- How often does the process create mistakes?
- What does fixing each mistake involve?
- Does the process delay invoicing or cash collection?
- Are leads or customers lost because follow-up is slow?
- Does the process require a senior employee to do low-value admin?
- Does the business need to hire sooner because volume creates more administration?

You do not need to force a dollar value onto every item. The goal is to understand whether the operational impact is material.

## Rank processes before automating them

Create a simple list and score each process from 1 to 5 for:

- frequency
- time consumed
- error rate
- delay created
- number of people involved
- customer impact
- ease of standardising the process

The highest-scoring process is usually a better place to start than whichever automation idea sounds most advanced. ## When this is not the answer

Do not automate a process just because the spreadsheet says it costs money.

A manual process may still be the better option when:

- it is highly variable
- it happens infrequently
- the human interaction creates value
- the process is likely to change soon
- the automation would introduce more risk than it removes
- the task is already simple and fast

Automation should create a better process, not simply a more technical one.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I know if a manual process is worth automating?

Look at frequency, time, predictability and business impact. A repetitive process that happens often and follows clear rules is usually a stronger candidate than a complex task performed occasionally.

### Should I include employee salary when calculating the cost?

Use a loaded hourly cost that makes sense for your business. The point is not to create a perfect accounting calculation. It is to estimate the operational effort currently being consumed.

### What if the process involves several departments?

Map the full workflow and measure each handoff. Cross-department processes often contain more waiting and duplicate entry than the individual teams realise.

### What if the manual process is caused by disconnected software?

That may be an integration problem rather than an automation problem. If both systems work well but information has to be copied between them, [connecting the systems](/services/system-integrations) can be the simplest fix.

## Document one workflow from trigger to completion

Choose one process your team complains about and write down every step, system, handoff and decision involved.

Then calculate the approximate time spent on the repetitive parts.

That gives you a much stronger basis for deciding what to improve than starting with an automation tool or an AI idea. If you are new to the idea, [what business process automation is](/blog/what-is-business-process-automation) is a good place to start.
